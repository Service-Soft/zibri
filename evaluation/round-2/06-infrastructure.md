## MAJOR

### F1. A holder never learns that its grant lapsed, so a store outage longer than one lease duplicates every running thread job and event hook — MAJOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`LeaseService.runRenewed` renews on a timer and `tryRenew` only logs when a renewal fails (`packages/zibri/src/leases/lease.service.ts:505-539`); the JSDoc at `:493-499` says so deliberately: "Losing the grant is the renewal's business to report, not this method's to act on: stopping halfway would leave whatever the work had already written behind, which is not obviously better than finishing." Nothing on `HeldLease` or in `withLease`'s callback carries an `AbortSignal` or any other way for the work to find out (`leases/held-lease.model.ts`, `leases/lease-service.interface.ts`). `docs/leases.md:157-159` documents the behaviour.

The recorded reason holds for a hook with partial writes. It does not hold for the two consumers that are *re-run from scratch* by whoever takes the lapsed grant. A thread job's claim is `jobClaimInMs` = 30 s by default (`di/default/zibri-di-providers.default.ts:266`), renewed every 10 s; a data source blip of 30 s loses every in-flight claim on every instance. When the store returns, the next poll on another instance finds the rows `IN_PROGRESS` with no live claim (`multithreading/services/multithreading.service.ts:96-106, 863-882`) and starts them again, while the original workers are still running — `holdAndRun` (`:890-912`) is never told anything, so the worker keeps going, both instances write `progress`, `status` and `result` to the same row (`:1053-1056, 1139-1145`) and last writer wins. An event hook is the same with `EVENT_EXECUTION_LEASE_IN_MS` = 1 min (`event.service.ts:448-464`): the new claimant runs the hook a second time and both `recordResult`s upsert the same ledger row. A cron slot grant is never renewed at all (F2). The docs promise "exactly one instance" for thread jobs (`docs/multithreading.md:118`) and "once across the deployment" for events (`docs/events.md:103`); a one-minute Postgres failover breaks both for everything that was running.

What makes this an enterprise concern rather than an edge: a managed database failover is routinely 30-90 s, the defaults are 30 s and 60 s, and the duplicate is silent — the only trace is a `warn` line per renewal that nobody alerts on (F9). The fix direction is small: give the work a signal that fires when a renewal reports a lost grant, let the thread job service terminate the worker on it (it already has `worker.terminate()` for timeouts, `:1064-1076`) and hand the hook's signal to the subscriber the way the timeout already does (`:457`). The alternative — fencing tokens checked on every write — is the full solution from 10 "C. Make the grant checkable at the end" and much larger.

**Verify:** Cluster suite under `packages/zibri/src/__testing__/evaluation/cluster/`: two instances, `jobClaimInMs: 2000`, queue a worker file that reports progress every 500 ms for 20 s; after it starts on instance A, make the lease table unreachable for 5 s (pause the container, or point A's renewals at a dropped connection) and assert that instance B never starts the same job id while A's worker is alive, i.e. the row's `startedAtMs` is written once. Same shape for an event hook with `EVENT_EXECUTION_LEASE_IN_MS: 2000`, asserting one invocation of the hook per `(event, subscriber)`.

> **Closed 2026-10-22.** The signal variant. The work under `withLease`, `withLeases` and `hold` is handed an `AbortSignal`, and `keepAlive` puts one on the `KeptGrant`, that aborts with `LeaseLostError` once the grant is lost: at once when `renew` finds the row gone (the built-in `renew` now throws `LeaseLostError` for that), and otherwise when a renewal fails after the grant's own lifetime has passed by the holder's clock, which is the store-outage case - a renewal that fails before then is still the logged warning and nothing more, since two of the three renewals per lease may fail without the grant lapsing. Nothing stops the work for it, as the recorded reason says; the two consumers that are re-run from scratch by whoever takes the grant act on it. The thread job pool settles the job and terminates its worker (`stopLostJob`), so the exit is a worker dying without a job - a replacement starts, nothing more is written to a row that belongs to the taker, and a `runThreadJob` waiting here goes on waiting for the row. The event hook gets the signal through the one it already has for the timeout (`withTimeout`'s third argument), and when it fires the result is not recorded: the taker runs the hook again and writes the ledger row. Found on the way: `EventServiceInterface.subscribe`/`subscribeAll` declared the hook without the signal the service passes and the docs promise, so a typed subscriber could not take it; the interface names it now. Tests, all in memory: `lease.service.test.ts` "losing a grant" (row taken away → `LeaseLostError` within a renewal; `updateAll` rejected throughout → not told at half a lease, told after a full one; `withLeases` and `hold` hand the signal), `multithreading.service.lost-claim.test.ts` (an endless worker whose claim is taken by "another instance" that restarts the row: after two claim lifetimes the row still says the taker's thread id, start time, progress 0 and attempts, and the next job runs on the replacement worker - red before: the first worker went on writing progress), `event.service.lost-claim.test.ts` (the hook's signal aborts with `LeaseLostError`, no `EventSubscriberRun` row, the event stays `CREATED`, and the next `dispatchPending` runs the hook again and records it). The two-process shape of the Verify, with the store paused rather than the row taken, is the user's cluster suite to run.

> **Completed 2026-10-23.** The closure above left a window: the signal fires at the renewal that finds the claim gone, up to a third of a claim after the takeover, and until then the worker's reports went on landing on the taker's row. The lost-claim test's sleep of two claim lifetimes hid it in isolation and showed it under the load of `test:normal` (progress 4 on the taker's row). Every write of a run is now a compare and set on the pair its start wrote, `attempts` and `startedAtMs` (`findRunCondition`, passed as `updateById`'s `where`); a write that matches nothing is a run another instance replaced, and lands on nothing. The write that starts a run is the one unconditioned, made under the claim just taken. The shutdown's `INTERRUPTED` write carries the same condition: unconditioned, it handed the job of a live instance to the next one to look. Test: `multithreading.service.lost-claim.test.ts` "before its next renewal", with a one-minute claim so no renewal falls inside it (red before: progress 6 on the taker's row half a second after the takeover; green after, and the row unchanged by `onAppShutdown`). The event hook has the same window, narrower in effect: a hook that finishes inside it records its ledger row, and the taker runs it again and records the same row.
>
> **Fixed 2026-10-23.** The compare and set broke two tests of `multithreading.service.test.ts` on Postgres, never in memory. `startJob` posted the job to the worker and armed its timeout before it wrote the start. So whatever the run reported before that write landed was checked against a row that did not have the run's pair yet, matched nothing, and was dropped as another instance's run. That included a progress report made at once (the row went from 0 to 100), the TimeoutError of a one millisecond timeout, and, with a slower write, the FAILED ending itself, which left the row `IN_QUEUE` with no claim. The start is now written before the worker gets the job. A pool that stopped, or a claim that was lost, while the write was out leaves the job unposted: settled by then, it would otherwise arm a timeout that later writes to a row this instance no longer holds. Test: `multithreading.service.slow-start-write.test.ts`, in memory, with the start write held back 300 ms as a round trip holds it. The early progress is kept and the one millisecond timeout is recorded (both red before: 100, and `IN_QUEUE`). After `onAppShutdown` during the held write, the row gets no TimeoutError (red after the reorder alone).

### F2. A cron run that outlives `CRON_EXECUTION_LEASE_IN_MS` is run again by the next pod that boots with `catchUpWithinMs` set — MAJOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`CronJob.runOnTick` writes `lastRun` only after `resolveTickOutcome` returns (`cron/cron-job.model.ts:568-584`), retries included. The slot grant is `cron:<job>:<slot>` with `onCompletion: 'retain'` and is never renewed (`cron/cron.service.ts:203-219`, comment at `:208-210`). `catchUpMissedRun` runs on every boot (`:113-161`): it reads `lastRun`, walks back for the most recent slot after it, and claims that slot's key with the same `shouldRunExecution`. A lapsed grant is deleted by `tryAcquire` (`leases/lease.service.ts:267`), so the claim succeeds.

Put together: a nightly job starts at 03:00 and takes 20 minutes; a rolling deploy at 03:08 boots new pods; the 03:00 grant lapsed at 03:05 (`CRON_EXECUTION_LEASE_IN_MS` = 5 min, `zibri-di-providers.default.ts:220`), `lastRun` still says yesterday, so each new pod finds 03:00 "missed", claims it and runs the job a second time beside the first — the "invoice run bills every customer twice" the docs use as the motivating example. `docs/cron.md:223-229` says to raise the lease "for jobs that legitimately run longer than the default", but the token is deployment-wide and a job's duration grows with its data, so the setting is wrong the first night it is exceeded; and the doc's reason for not renewing ("node-cron owns the job body and only asks who should run") is not accurate — `runOnTick` *is* the framework's wrapper around the body and runs for the whole duration, so renewing is possible there.

Fix direction: record that a run has started (a `runStartedAt` on the row, or renew the slot grant for the duration of `runOnTick` by returning the held grant from `shouldRunExecution` instead of a boolean) and let catch-up skip a slot whose run is in progress. Both make the lease length irrelevant to correctness, which is what the rest of the lease fold achieved for mail, events and thread jobs.

This is the infrastructure instance of the class round-2 05 §I9 files for auth (a lease duration shorter than, or unrelated to, the work it guards): the cron slot grant is the one lease in the framework whose duration has to be *chosen* against the work, and the one whose default, five minutes, is below a routine nightly job. Every other claim in my area (mail `sendLeaseInMs` 1 min, event 1 min, thread job 30 s, payment transition 30 s) is renewed under the work, so its duration only decides takeover latency and the ordering problem does not arise there.

**Verify:** `startTestServer` with the in-memory data source and a job whose `onTick` waits 3 s, `catchUpWithinMs: Ms.DAY`, `CRON_EXECUTION_LEASE_IN_MS` overridden to 500 ms; fire the slot, then after 1 s boot a second application against the same data source (`StartedTestServer.reInit()` or the cluster helper) and assert the job's `onTick` ran once across both. It runs twice today.

> **Closed 2026-10-22.** The renewal variant: the slot grant now follows the work, as every other claim in the framework does. node-cron's own `distributed`/`runCoordinator` election is no longer used, because it elects before the body and holds nothing across it; instead `CronJob.runOnTick(scheduledAt)` runs the tick of a distributed job inside `runExecution(key, lease, run)` of the cron service, which is `withLease({ mode: 'skip', onCompletion: 'retain' }, run)` - renewed underneath the run, kept afterwards. `ShouldRunExecution` became `RunExecution` (`(key, ttlMs, run) => Promise<boolean>`, the renamed `runExecution` on `CronServiceInterface`; the harness stub runs what it is handed), the startup claim passes a no-op run, and `catchUpMissedRun` no longer claims the slot itself: it calls `runOnTick(missed)`, so a make-up run competes for the slot's own key and is skipped while another instance is still running it, however long that takes. The lease length is now only takeover latency and how long a finished slot stays claimed; the docs say so instead of "the one lease Zibri does not renew". On the way: a `distributed: false` job's missed slot is now caught up on every instance (the election no longer applies to it), which was 06 §F14. Tests: `cron-job.held-slot.test.ts` (in memory, lease 500 ms, run 1.5 s): a second claim of the slot during the run, after the lease would have lapsed unrenewed, does not run it again, and the row says the slot ran; a finished slot stays claimed, and is taken again once the lease lapsed, which is the takeover of a dead instance. The on-failure suite clears the slot claims between its cases, which reuse the same upcoming slots. The cross-process shape of the finding's Verify, two instances on one store, is the user's cluster suite to run.

### F3. The event dispatch poll reads the oldest 50 `CREATED` events, so 50 events waiting on a long retry delay stop every event behind them from being dispatched — MAJOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`EventService.dispatchPending` loads `status: CREATED`, `take: EVENT_MAX_CONCURRENT_HOOKS` (50), ordered by `createdAt ASC` (`event/event.service.ts:677-683`). An event with a `PENDING_RETRY` run stays `CREATED` (`:545-548, 705-706`), is read on every poll, offered, and skipped by `owesRun` until `nextRetryAt` (`:745-755`). Nothing in the query excludes it. The retry delay doubles from one minute (`:571-573`), so a subscriber with `attempts: 6` sits in the page for up to 16 minutes per attempt; with `attempts: 10` for hours.

So once the fifty oldest `CREATED` rows are all waiting for a retry, the poll's page is full of events it will not run and every younger event is never read. The emitting process still delivers in-process (`:183`), which hides this on a single-tier deployment — but on the "web emits, worker handles" topology `docs/events.md:117-125` describes, and whenever the emitting instance is at `EVENT_MAX_CONCURRENT_HOOKS` (`:369-375`) or died, the poll is the only delivery path and it is stalled. An upstream outage that fails fifty webhook deliveries therefore stops every *other* event type in the system for as long as the backoff of those fifty runs, with nothing logged, because from the poll's point of view there is nothing to do.

The fix is in the query: exclude events whose only outstanding runs have `nextRetryAt` in the future (a join on the runs, or a `nextDispatchAt` column on `Event` maintained by `recordResult`), and page past them. `04 §2.3/2.4` closed the "where to put a failed run" question; the poll was not revisited for it.

**Verify:** `startTestServer` (in-memory data source), `EVENT_MAX_CONCURRENT_HOOKS: 1`, `EVENT_RETRY_DELAY_IN_MS: Ms.HOUR`. Subscribe `a` with `attempts: 2` and a hook that throws; subscribe `b` with a hook that records; emit one `a` event (it fails and goes `PENDING_RETRY`). Then occupy the single hook slot with a never-resolving hook `c`, emit a `b` event (refused in process, `:369-375`), release `c`, and assert `b`'s hook runs within three dispatch ticks. It never runs: the page of size 1 holds the `a` event forever.

> **Closed 2026-10-22.** The column variant: `Event.nextDispatchAt` (default now, left out of `EventCreateData`), which the poll filters on with `beforeOrOn: now` beside `status: CREATED`. `recordResult` no longer returns early on `PENDING_RETRY`: every result goes through `settleEvent`, and an event that is not settled gets `scheduleNextDispatch` - the earliest due time over the subscribers it still owes (now for one that never ran, its `nextRetryAt` for one waiting, nothing for one that ended), so an event with one subscriber retrying and another never run stays due now. `replayFailed` sets the events it puts back due now. The poll itself writes nothing extra: the reschedule runs once per hook result, not once per poll. The first run of the new suite beside the tenancy suite showed `before` refusing an event polled in the millisecond it was created, hence `beforeOrOn`. Test: `event.service.retry-backlog.test.ts` (in memory, one hook slot, an hour's retry): the oldest event fails and is deferred by about an hour, a blocking hook takes the slot so the next emit is left to the poll, and once the slot is free three polls reach the younger event - which a page of one holding the deferred event never did. The replay, tenancy and cleanup suites still pass, replay's "offers a failed run again" being the case that proves a deferred event comes back when its retry is due.

### F4. The thread job table is never cleaned up, declares no index, and is scanned by every instance every two seconds — MAJOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

Every finished job keeps its row: there is no cleanup cron job for `ThreadJobEntity` (every other queue has one — `email-cleanup`, `event-cleanup`, `lease-cleanup`, `log-cleanup`), nothing deletes `COMPLETED`/`FAILED`/`CANCELLED` rows, and the rows carry the full `workerData` and `result` as json columns (`multithreading/models/thread-job-entity.model.ts:93-98`). `candidatesFor` runs `findAll({ where: { status: { oneOf: [...] }, priority }, order: { queuedAtMs: 'ASC' }, take: 10 })` once per priority class per selection round (`multithreading.service.ts:826-850`), and a round runs on every instance every 2 s (`run-queued-thread-jobs.cron-job.ts:27`), after every completion and after every queue call. The entity declares no `indexes` although `@Entity` supports them (`entity/decorators/entity.decorator.ts:78, 161`; only the SCIM models use it). On postgres that is a sequential scan with a sort over a table that only grows, from N instances, 30 times a minute each, returning rows whose payloads can be large.

An enterprise reviewer reads this as "the job table is a time bomb": a deployment that queues ten thousand jobs a day has four million rows after a year, every poll gets slower, and the only remedy is a hand-written cron job against a framework-owned table. The retention question has the same answer the mail and event queues already have (a required `RetentionInMs`), and the index is one line on the decorator. The same gap exists for `Email` (`status`, `priority`, `retryAt`, polled every 5 s, `email.service.ts:350-362`), `Event` (`status`, polled every 5 s) and `CacheInvalidation` (full table every 2 s) — none declares an index beyond what `unique` gives.

**Verify:** A unit test over the model registry asserting `ThreadJobEntity`, `Email`, `Event` and `CacheInvalidation` declare an index covering the columns their poll filters on; and a `startTestServer` test that runs 100 jobs to completion, restarts through the real lifecycle twice, and asserts the `ThreadJobEntity` row count is bounded by a retention option — it is 100 today.

> **Closed 2026-10-22.** Retention is the required token `ZIBRI_DI_TOKENS.THREAD_JOB_RETENTION_IN_MS` (`RetentionInMs`, the same shape the mail queue takes; `MultithreadingService.onAppInit` refuses to start without it, the scaffold and sandbox say `'forever'`). `ThreadJobCleanupCronJob` runs daily and calls `deleteExpiredJobs`, which deletes `COMPLETED`/`FAILED`/`CANCELLED` rows across tenants whose `queuedAtMs` is older than the retention — a job still queued, running or interrupted is kept however old. The four polled entities declare the index their poll filters on: `ThreadJobEntity` `[status, priority, queuedAtMs]`, `Email` `[status, priority, retryAt]`, `Event` `[status, nextDispatchAt]`, `CacheInvalidation` `[at]`. `entity/polled-entities.indexes.test.ts` is the first Verify; `multithreading/thread-job-cleanup.cron-job.test.ts` fills the queue with rows in every status and age and runs the cron job by hand (the row-count-after-two-restarts form of the second Verify is replaced by that, because the harness runs no cron jobs). Postgres schemas gain the indexes on the next sync.

### F5. A thread job that kills its host is taken over and re-run without limit, and workers run without resource limits — MAJOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`new Worker(this.threadJobWorkerFilePath, { workerData: {} })` (`multithreading.service.ts:353`) passes no `resourceLimits`, so a worker's heap is bounded only by the container. A job that allocates until the cgroup kills the *process* writes nothing (the graceful path at `:694-720` never runs), its claim lapses, and `CLAIMABLE_STATUSES` includes `IN_PROGRESS` (`:102-106`) so the next instance takes it over and dies the same way. Nothing counts takeovers: `startJob` resets `progress: 0` and `startedAtMs` (`:1053-1056`), there is no `attempts` on the row, no dead-letter status, and `docs/multithreading.md:127-131` describes the takeover as a feature without naming the loop.

That is the textbook poison message, and in this design it is a poison *pod*: one bad input in a report-rendering job walks the whole deployment through OOM kills, one pod every `jobClaimInMs`. A worker-level `resourceLimits.maxOldGenerationSizeMb` turns the same job into `ERR_WORKER_OUT_OF_MEMORY`, which the service already handles as a worker exit with a `FAILED` row (`:1317-1339`); a takeover counter with a cap turns the remaining cases (native crash, host kill) into a terminal status an operator can see. Both are small; the second needs one column.

**Verify:** `startTestServer` with the in-memory data source: queue a worker file that fills an array until it throws, with a `resourceLimits` of 64 MB passed through `MultithreadingOptions` (new option), and assert the row ends `FAILED` with the out-of-memory error and the pool replaces the worker. For the counter: plant an `IN_PROGRESS` row with a lapsed claim, let the instance take it over three times by terminating the worker from the test, and assert the fourth poll leaves it in a terminal status rather than starting it again.

> **Closed 2026-10-22.** `MultithreadingOptions` gained `workerResourceLimits` (node's `ResourceLimits`, handed to every `Worker`; the default provider gives `maxOldGenerationSizeMb` an even share of `process.constrainedMemory()` or the machine, split between the workers and the main thread) and `maxAttempts` (default 3, refused below 1 at init). `ThreadJobEntity.attempts` counts the starts: `startJob` writes `attempts + 1`, `requeueThreadJob` resets it, and the claim callback fails a row at `maxAttempts` with `ThreadJobAttemptsExhaustedError` (status `FAILED`, `error` on the row, `onError` called, logged) without starting it — the worker goes on to the next candidate as if the claim was somebody else's. A worker past the heap limit raises `ERR_WORKER_OUT_OF_MEMORY`, which the existing error handler already writes as the job's `FAILED` error before the exit replaces the worker. `multithreading.service.limits.test.ts` (in memory, compiled fixtures so the limit is on the job's heap alone): the allocating worker ends `FAILED` with the memory error and the next job runs on the replacement; a planted abandoned row at `maxAttempts - 1` is taken over and completes at `maxAttempts`; one at `maxAttempts` is failed with the error, `startedAtMs` and `progress` untouched; a requeue starts the count at 1 again. The Verify's three real takeovers are the same code path exercised with a planted count, since a worker terminated from the test ends the job `FAILED` rather than leaving it `IN_PROGRESS`. The upgrade migration adds `attempts` (and `Event.nextDispatchAt`, which §F3 had left out of it). `docs/multithreading.md` names both options and the bound under "Surviving a restart".

### F6. A mail attachment is a path on the queueing instance's disk, and any instance may send the mail — MAJOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`EmailAttachment` is `{ filename, path: FsPath }` (`email/models/email-attachment.model.ts:7-19`). The queue is a shared table and `SendQueuedEmailsCronJob` is `distributed: true` by default, so whichever instance wins the 5-second slot drains the queue (`email/send-queued-emails.cron-job.ts`, `email.service.ts:164-220`). `send` first runs `validateAttachments`, which checks `FsUtilities.exists(a.path)` on *the sending instance* (`:295-296, 330-342`) and throws otherwise; the throw is counted as an attempt and retried with doubling delay until `maxSendAttempts` (`:241-273`), after which the row is `FAILED` and never attempted again.

On two or more pods an invoice mail queued by pod A with a pdf under A's `.zibri/uploads` is sent by pod B with probability roughly 1 - 1/N, fails five times over 31 minutes, and ends `FAILED` with a "does not exist" error — a silent, ordinary-looking failure that the single-instance test suite can never produce. `docs/email.md` does not mention it (line 130 lists "an attachment is missing" as a transport error); `docs/replication.md` lists what works across instances and does not list attachments. The fix needs a decision: store the attachment content (or an asset reference the asset service can resolve on any instance) on the row, or pin a mail with attachments to the queueing instance by recording its identity — which the lease model has no notion of today (F12).

**Verify:** Cluster suite with two instances sharing the in-memory or postgres data source: queue a mail with an attachment that exists only on instance A's staging folder, disable A's `SendQueuedEmailsCronJob`, and assert the mail is sent. It ends `FAILED` after `maxSendAttempts`.

> **Closed 2026-10-22.** Decided for content on the row: pinning needs an instance identity the lease model lacks (§F12), and strands the mail when its instance dies. `EmailAttachment` is `{ filename, base64Content }`; `path` is gone, and so is `validateAttachments`, since nothing on a disk is read any more. `SmtpEmailTransport` hands nodemailer `{ filename, content, encoding: 'base64' }`. Red first: `email.service.attachments.test.ts` (in memory) found the content missing from the stored row, and the new case in `smtp.email-transport.test.ts` found it missing from the message on the wire. The two path cases of `email.service.test.ts` moved into the in-memory suite, and the retries case that used a missing attachment as its failing send is gone with the failure. The Verify runs as the last case of `mail.cluster.test.ts`: instance 0 queues an attachment mail, refuses every send from then on, and is killed; another instance sends the mail with the decoded content intact. `disable` is stored and applies to every instance, so the refusal stands in for it, and `retryDelayInMs` in that suite is one second so a refused first attempt is retried within the test. The upgrade migration reads the file of each queued 2.x attachment into `base64Content`, and fails the mail when the file cannot be read. `docs/email.md` gained "Attaching files", and `docs/replication.md` lists attachments among what works across instances.

### F7. The logger records `originalUrl` with its query string on every entry of a request, so secrets carried in links land in the log table — MAJOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

Every log line written while a request is handled gets `request.url = requestContext.request.originalUrl` (`logging/logger.ts:240-256`), and the access log writes `req.originalUrl` into both the message and the context (`:133-143`). `LogRequestContext.url` is a stored column (`logging/log-context.model.ts:45-48`) and the db transport is a default transport at `INFO` with a two-week retention. The framework's own routes put secrets in the query string and the path: the mailing-list links carry `?subscriberId=…&secret=…`, where `secret` is the subscriber's permanent credential (`plugin/mailing-list/services/mailing-list.service.ts:142-144`, controller `:94-152`), the subscribe link carries the confirmation token in the path (`:71`), and the federated callbacks arrive with `?code=&state=` (`auth/authenticators/federated/federated-route.utilities.ts:158,172`). A refused link is an `UnauthorizedError`, whose audit entry (`logging/logger.ts:217-224`) carries that request context — so the failure path logs the presented secret even with the access log off, and the success path does whenever anything inside logs at `INFO`.

`docs/tracing.md:218` states the opposite policy for spans — "records a url without its query string for that reason" — and `http-client.ts:396-399` does strip it for outgoing calls; the logger is the one place the rule was not applied. ### F8. The http client reads a non-ok response body unbounded — regression of 04 §4.2 on the error path — MAJOR

### F25. The audit trail rides the ordinary log pipeline, where it is the first thing dropped and the first thing collapsed — MAJOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.* Cross-reference round-2 04 (auth-core), which raised the question; this is the logging-side judgement.

`Logger.audit` writes an `INFO` entry on success and a `WARN` on failure through the same transports as everything else (`logging/logger.ts:217-224`). Three properties of that pipeline work against an audit trail. The buffer evicts the *least important* entry when full (`logging/log-buffer.ts:13-21, 91-112`), so under pressure — which is exactly when a credential-stuffing run happens — successful-login audit entries at `INFO` are the first to go, and a dropped batch is recorded only as a count (`logger.ts:367-376`). The db transport's budget collapses entries of one kind past 1000 a minute into one row with a count (`logging/transport/log-budget.ts:140-175`), and `docs/logging.md:86` says explicitly that a run of refused logins against one address becomes one row — which is the per-attempt record (source address, timestamp, reason) a SIEM correlates on. And retention is the level's `LOGGER_CLEANUP_AFTER_MS` (`:262`), two weeks by the docs' own statement at `docs/logging.md:102-104`, where an enterprise's audit retention is counted in years and is append-only.

The documented seam is "replace the logger" (`docs/logging.md:92-98`), which means re-implementing the buffer, the batching and the transports to change one method's routing. The deliberate reasoning is written down and is sound for *logs*; it is insufficient for an *audit trail* because the three properties above are the opposite of what one needs: never dropped, never collapsed, retained separately. Fix direction: give `audit` its own queue and transport list (`ZIBRI_DI_TOKENS.AUDIT_TRANSPORTS`, defaulting to the db transport without a budget and with its own retention), keep the copy on the ordinary transports for readability, and leave tamper evidence (a hash chain per tenant) as a documented option.

**Verify:** `startTestServer` with `LOGGER_BUFFER_CAPACITY: 5`: log six `debug` lines, then one `audit` success, then flood with twenty `WARN` lines before the flush; assert the audit row exists after `flush()` (it is evicted today). Second test: 1200 refused logins in one minute against one address, assert 1200 audit rows (there are 1000 plus one collapsed row today).

> **Closed 2026-10-22.** The audit trail is a subsystem of its own, next to the logs rather than instead of them. `AuditServiceInterface` (`ZIBRI_DI_TOKENS.AUDIT_SERVICE`, default `AuditService`) has `record(event)`, `flush()` and `deleteExpiredEntries()`, and stores a typed `AuditEntry` table: action, outcome, reason, subject, user, role, scopes, tenant, client address, trace id, `occurredAt`/`lastOccurredAt`, `count` and `collapsed`. Every one of the 56 framework call sites records through it. `record` also writes the log line, unchanged (`info`/`warn` with the event under `context.metadata.audit`). `LoggerInterface.audit` is removed by the user's call, which makes the audit service the one entry point and the place to replace.
>
> The second Verify case (1200 rows) was not taken as written. The user pointed out that an audit trail which never drops anything and has no budget hands a flood the write rate. The design splits by who decides the volume instead:
> - **Successes** (something passed a check, so legitimate activity and rate limits bound their volume) are never collapsed and never dropped.
> - **Refusals** (the caller decides their volume) are entries of their own up to `AUDIT_MAX_REFUSALS_PER_MINUTE` (1000 by default) per instance. Past that they are collapsed per kind (tenant, action, reason, subject, user, role, scopes, client address) into one entry with a count. At most 100 kinds are kept apart; the rest are counted per action and reason in an entry of no tenant. A refusal is always counted.
>
> The audit budget is separate from the log's, so log noise never uses it up. Nothing waits on the trail: entries are written at once, and what arrives during a write shares the next one. An entry the data source refuses stays pending and is retried after 5 s. `audit_entries_pending` is a gauge, and shutdown flushes.
>
> Retention is `AUDIT_RETENTION_IN_MS`, required like `THREAD_JOB_RETENTION_IN_MS`, with `AuditCleanupCronJob` deleting by `lastOccurredAt`. The scaffold, the sandbox and the zibri-testing providers set one year (the user asked for a concrete default over `'forever'`). `AuditEntry` was added to `zibriFrameworkEntities`, `defaultTestServerEntities`, the sandbox, the scaffold and its parity fixture, and to the upgrade migration.
>
> The log budget's collapsing became the generic `WriteBudget<T>` (`utilities/write-budget.ts`), which `LogBudget` and `AuditBudget` extend.
>
> Found on the way and fixed, red first: a log entry of no tenant was stored under whichever tenant's entry had scheduled the write. The create path fills an `undefined` tenant from the context the write runs in, so the entry was hidden from every other tenant. `Logger`, the dropped-entries notice, `LogBudget`'s overflow row and the audit entries now name no tenant with `null` (new case in `logger.tenancy.test.ts`).
>
> Tamper evidence (a hash chain) was split out by the user's call, since it is a property of a table rather than of auditing, and is filed as F26.
>
> Tests: `audit.service.test.ts`, 8 cases, in memory:
> - a success survives the evicting log buffer;
> - refused logins over http carry their address and are counted per kind past a budget of 3;
> - successes are never collapsed;
> - 1200 refusals of distinct addresses add up to 1200 across singles, 100 kinds and one overflow entry;
> - a refused write is kept, reported pending, and stored on the next flush;
> - retention;
> - two startup refusals.
>
> Docs: a new `audit.md`; `logging.md` "Security events"; the auth, error-handling and metrics links and rows; the upgrade guide (retention and entity, migration `createEntity`, "Audit events" under your own code).

*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`maxThreads = Math.max(1, allThreads - UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE - 2)` with `allThreads = os.availableParallelism()` (`di/default/zibri-di-providers.default.ts:71-79`). Node's answer comes from the scheduler affinity mask, not from the cgroup cpu quota, so a pod limited to 1 CPU on a 64-core node starts 58 worker threads at boot (`multithreading.service.ts:311-316`), each a V8 isolate, and `validateInputs` is satisfied. `docs/replication.md:107-116` names the N-replicas-per-host case and says to set it explicitly; the single-pod-with-a-quota case is the common one and is not named. Fix: read `/sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.max` when present, or default to a small constant and let the deployment raise it.

**Verify:** Unit test of the default factory with `os.availableParallelism` stubbed to 64 and a stubbed `cpu.max` of `100000 100000`; assert `maxThreads` is 1.

### F17. A mail batch larger than `maxEmailsPerHour` is never sent — MINOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`sendMany` asks the token bucket for `emails.length` tokens at once (`email.service.ts:230-233`); `consume` allows only when `refilled.tokens >= count` and the bucket never holds more than `capacity` (`rate-limiting/limiter/token-bucket.rate-limiter.ts:73, 152`). With `sendBatchSize` above `maxEmailsPerHour` — `{ maxEmailsPerHour: 5 }` with the default batch of 10, or a batch raised to 100 against a 50/hour provider — a queue holding at least a batch's worth of mails is refused on every pass forever, returns `false` (`:208-211`), and nothing is logged. Fix: cap the batch at the limiter's capacity, or refuse the configuration at boot.

**Verify:** `startTestServer` with `maxEmailsPerHour: 5`, `sendBatchSize: 10`, queue 10 mails with a recording transport, run the job for three ticks, assert at least 5 were sent. None are.

### F18. A payment the buyer never approves stays `CREATED` forever, and there is no provider-side reconciliation — MINOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`startOrder` writes the row and the PayPal order (`pay-pal.payment-provider.ts:294-332`); confirmation only happens when the application calls `confirmPayment`. No cron job expires abandoned `CREATED` rows, no webhook endpoint exists in the plugin (grep `webhook` under `plugin/` finds nothing), so an order PayPal auto-voids after its approval window keeps a live-looking row with a `transactionId` that can never be reused (unique, `payment.model.ts:25-26`). The idempotency keys and `reconcile` path for a lost answer are good (`:557-588`); what is missing is the time axis. Fix: a plugin cron job that asks `getOrder` for `CREATED` payments older than the provider's window and moves them to `CANCELLED`.

**Verify:** `startTestServer` with a fake PayPal client: start a payment, advance the clock a week, run the (new) reconciliation job, assert the row is `CANCELLED` and the `transactionId` can be used again.

### F19. A backup or restore is guarded per process only — MINOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`isCreatingBackup` and `isRestoringBackup` are `BehaviorSubject`s on the service (`backup/backup.service.ts:231-234`), and `restore()`/`createBackup()` take no lease (grep `withLease` in the file finds none). Two instances can restore at once, or one can restore while another's scheduled backup reads the resources being replaced; the shutdown wait at `:306-307` only covers this process. The content side is another reviewer's; from the lease side the fix is one `withLease({ key: 'backup', mode: 'wait' })` around both, which also gives the operator the "is one running anywhere" answer.

**Verify:** Cluster suite: start a restore on instance A against a slow transport, call `restore` on B, assert B waits or refuses rather than running concurrently.

### F20. The http client follows redirects unconditionally and has no `HEAD`/`OPTIONS` — MINOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`fetch` is called without `redirect` (`http-client.ts:1039-1042`), so every call follows up to 20 redirects including cross-origin and https-to-http ones; the circuit breaker, the `server.address` attribute and the retry policy are keyed on the *original* origin (`:400, 980-986`), so a dependency that redirects to a failing host never opens its circuit. There is no option to set `redirect: 'manual' | 'error'`, which is what a webhook sender or anything fetching a customer-supplied url needs against SSRF via redirect. `HEAD`, `OPTIONS` and `TRACE` throw "Not implemented yet." (`:383-385`), which a client that validates response headers would reasonably be used for.

**Verify:** `startTestServer` with a route redirecting to a second route; `get` with a new `redirect: 'error'` option asserts a thrown `HttpClientError` with status 302.

### F21. Console logging is human-formatted only, and levels are per transport rather than per origin — MINOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`logToConsole` prints coloured multi-line text (`logging/transport/log-to-console.function.ts`); there is no JSON-lines transport, which is what every stdout log shipper (Loki, Datadog, CloudWatch, Splunk) expects, and the db transport is the only structured sink. A transport is five lines through `LoggerTransport.create`, so this is DX rather than capability — but the generated project's default is the console transport, and an enterprise's first week with it is "write a transport". Level is a property of the transport (`BaseLoggerTransportConfig.level`), so a noisy module cannot be turned down without turning the whole transport down; `origin` is recorded on every entry (`logger.ts:231`) and would key a per-origin override cheaply.

**Verify:** A unit test that `LoggerTransport.json(LogLevel.INFO)` (new) writes one JSON object per entry with `level`, `message`, `traceId` and `origin`, parseable by `JSON.parse` per line.

### F23. An invoice's year is the server's local calendar year — MINOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`new Date().getFullYear()` in `generateInvoiceNumber`, `getTemporaryConsecutiveNumber` and `getConsecutiveNumber` (`plugin/invoicing/services/invoice-number.service.ts:55, 73, 107, 129`) reads the process time zone — UTC in every container — while the tenant's invoices are numbered per *its* fiscal year. An invoice issued at 00:30 on 1 January in Berlin draws last year's counter and number. The cron subsystem already solved the same question with `LOCALIZE_OPTIONS.defaultTimezone` and a per-job zone (`cron-job.model.ts:148-150`); the date-model rework is known to be deferred, this is one concrete consumer of it.

### F24. One worker thread runs many jobs and many tenants, and only module scope is reset between them — MINOR
*Filed 2026-10-21.*

`loadWorkerFile` re-evaluates the file per job by dropping it from `require.cache` or importing a fresh url (`multithreading/services/worker/load-worker-file.function.ts:75-95`); the comment at `thread-job.worker.ts:25-28` names the shared state that survives: "module scope, the `functionCache` below, anything a job left behind". `globalThis`, patched prototypes, `process.env` mutations and listeners on `parentPort` all survive from one tenant's job to the next tenant's on the same thread, and a `run()` function source is cached by its text (`:70-82`) across callers. For a single application this is a hygiene note; for a multi-tenant deployment where worker files handle untrusted input it is the isolation boundary, and the docs do not say it is a thread rather than a process. Fix direction: say so in `docs/multithreading.md`, and offer `resourceLimits` plus an opt-in "fresh worker per job" for sensitive work.

**Verify:** `startTestServer` test: a worker file that sets `globalThis.leak = workerData.tenantId`; queue it in tenant A, then in tenant B a file that reports `globalThis.leak`; assert the second reports `undefined` (it reports A today — a held expectation documenting the boundary, red until an isolation option exists).

### F26. Nothing tells an edited or deleted row of the audit trail apart from a recorded one — MINOR
*Filed 2026-10-22*, split out of F25 by the user's call: tamper evidence is a property of a table, not of auditing, so it is to be built as a capability of its own that the audit trail then uses.

Anyone who can write the `AuditEntry` table can change a subject, delete the refusals of one address or insert entries, and nothing that reads the trail can tell. An enterprise audit trail is expected to be append-only or at least tamper-evident. Fix direction: a hash chain per tenant (each row carries a sequence and an HMAC over its content plus the previous row's hash, keyed from the master key through the encryption service so that someone with database access alone cannot rewrite it), writes serialized per chain, and a `verify` that names the first broken row. No chain can catch rows cut from either end on its own, so `verify` returns the newest sequence and hash for the operator to keep outside the database. Retention removes rows from the old end, and the chain has to tell that apart from tampering.

**Verify:** `startTestServer` test, in memory: record three events in a tenant; change the `subject` of the second through the repository; assert that verifying the tenant's trail names the second entry. Then delete it and assert that verifying names the gap.

**Cost, measured before building (2026-10-22, at the user's request).**
- **Today:** a burst of 100 events across 10 tenants is stored in at most 2 writes (`audit.service.costs.test.ts`), and an uncontended lease costs 4 calls (`lease.service.costs.test.ts`).
- **Chain per tenant written inline:** each batch needs a lease, a head read and an insert per tenant in it, about 6 calls per tenant. The same burst becomes about 60 calls instead of 2. Every instance writing for one tenant queues on that tenant's lease, so the trail's write latency becomes the chain's, on the request-heavy path.
- **Cheaper shapes:**
  - (a) A chain per writing process: the head stays in memory, so there is no lease and no head read, and the cost stays about today's. The price is that a tenant's entries are spread over chains.
  - (b) Store as today and seal in the background: a polling job takes each tenant's unsealed entries in order under one lease, and chains and updates them in one batch. The cost is per active tenant per interval, not per event. The price is a window of unsealed entries.

  HMAC itself is microseconds per entry; the master key is unwrapped once per chain.
